# Optimal training plan

This skill is not a plan template. It's a process that takes you from a conversation with a person to a plan fitted to them: **collect data → find research → build the plan**. "Optimal" means two things at once: consistent with what research shows (volume, intensity, frequency, exercise selection, periodization, combining goals), and fitted to this specific person, their history, time, equipment and limitations. A plan from memory or "from a table" meets at most the first condition, so don't skip the interview or the research.

Reply and write the plan in the language the person writes in, in plain words, without assuming they know training jargon. Search for research in English, because that's where most of it is.

## Reference files

- `references/data-by-goal.md`: what data to collect (core and modules for motor qualities), what each item changes in the plan, how to ask, and proxy questions for estimating strength. **Read it before the interview** (step 3).
- `references/plan-updates.md`: how to change an existing plan. **Read it only when the person comes back with a plan** to change or asks for an adjustment. It isn't needed when building a new plan.

## Before you start

Check whether you have web search. If not, say so right at the start and suggest turning it on. Don't quietly build the plan from memory, because the person would think they're getting a plan based on current research.

## Flow

1. **Explanation level.** This is the only question at the start: does the person want no explanation, a short one (3–5 sentences), or a detailed one (every important decision explained) with the plan. The interview is always thorough, so don't ask about its length.
2. **Open statement.** Ask: "Tell me everything you want: your goal, how you train now, what gets in the way." Right away, also ask them to paste or describe their current plan, if they have one (a photo or a note works). The current plan is the best starting point, because it shows what the person actually does.
3. **Goal interpretation and summary.** Read `references/data-by-goal.md`. Break the goal into motor qualities (see "Goal interpretation" below), briefly list what you already know from their statement, and ask them to confirm your interpretation. Don't ask again about anything they've already said.
4. **Missing core data**, in batches of 3–5 questions.
5. **Modules** for the motor qualities in the goal. For a combined goal, gather questions from all needed modules without repeats.
6. **Weights.** Right away, ask for weight × reps from a few basic exercises that fit the goal. If they skip it, move to the proxy questions in `data-by-goal.md`.
7. **Your own questions.** The list in the file is a minimum. Think about what else you need to choose optimal parameters for this goal. For unusual or sport-specific goals, look up how specialists assess that quality (sport needs analysis per NSCA, standard fitness tests in that discipline, typical injuries) and derive questions from that.
8. **Skipped key data.** If the person skipped something marked as key, ask once at the end of the interview whether they're sure they want to skip it.
9. **Research → "research → decision" note → checklist → plan.**

With every batch of questions, note that any question can be skipped. A skipped item is "no data": don't make up a default value; work without that information and choose solutions that are reasonable without it. A made-up value would look like a fact about the person in the plan, and it isn't one.

## Goal interpretation

Every goal, including unusual and combined ones, can be broken into motor qualities: strength, power and explosiveness, hypertrophy, aerobic endurance, anaerobic and strength endurance, mobility, movement control and balance. Break it down separately by body region when the goal calls for it. Example: "bigger arms and dunking" is upper-body hypertrophy plus lower-body power and strength.

- **Main and secondary goals.** The main goal gets the most volume and the best spot in the week (e.g. jumps when fresh, at the start of a session). Secondary goals get enough volume to progress or maintain.
- **Conflicts.** Check whether the goals get in each other's way, e.g. heavy hypertrophy leg work the day before jumping, or high running volume alongside a strength goal. Base the solutions (separate days, order, lower volume) on research on interference.
- **Fat loss** isn't a motor quality. A training plan for that goal should protect muscle and strength and increase energy expenditure. Say honestly that diet has the biggest say in losing fat, and that this skill covers training only.
- **Confirmation.** Before asking for details, write how you understand the goal ("Here's how I understand it: … Is that right?"). A wrong interpretation ruins the whole plan, and fixing it at this stage is cheapest.

## Estimating strength

- When the person gives weight × reps, estimate their one-rep max (1RM) with Epley (1RM = weight × (1 + reps / 30)) or Brzycki (1RM = weight × 36 / (37 − reps)). Use them only for sets of up to about 10 reps; with longer sets they're inaccurate, so treat the result as rough. Work out training weights from the same formula in reverse, and convert between related exercises (e.g. dumbbell press from barbell press) cautiously.
- When the person doesn't give weights, estimate their general strength level from the proxy questions, sex, body weight and training experience. Estimate conservatively: too light a weight in the first week costs less than too heavy.
- Give weights in the table as ranges (e.g. 40–45 kg), rounded to real increments (a barbell usually in 2.5 kg steps, dumbbells in 1–2 kg steps). Use the units the person uses. For unloaded exercises write e.g. "body weight" or the box height.
- Before the table, show a short "Estimated level" block: the level (e.g. intermediate) and estimates for key exercises, marked as estimates.

## Research

1. **Research first, then the plan.** Don't build the plan from memory.
2. **Search plan.** For each motor quality in the goal, search separately for specific variables: volume, intensity, frequency, exercise selection, rest, order, combining goals (interference). Always account for this person's population (training experience, age, sex). For typical goals a shorter search is enough; for unusual ones, search deeper.
3. **Source hierarchy:** meta-analyses and systematic reviews → ACSM and NSCA position stands → single studies → blogs only with a note, when nothing better exists. Newer meta-analyses take precedence over older ones.
4. **"Research → decision" note.** Before building the table, prepare for each variable: what the research says, what range, what you choose for this person and why (history, current volume, time, preferences). Start close to the person's current volume rather than at a value "from a table", because a sudden jump in volume raises the risk of overload. The note is your working tool; the explanation is built from it, to the extent the person chose in step 1.
5. **Conflicting or weak evidence:** choose the best-documented option and say plainly where the evidence is weak. This matters especially for rare goals.
6. **Sources:** list the 2–5 most important ones under the plan.

## Checklist before handing over the plan

Go through it silently and fix the plan if anything fails:
- Do the sessions fit the time available?
- Are injuries and limitations accounted for in exercise selection?
- Does volume stay close to the current level rather than jumping sharply?
- Do the goals not clash (conflicts, interference)?
- Are the weights realistic for this person?
- Does every variable in the table have a justification in the "research → decision" note?

## Plan format

The plan usually covers 6–8 weeks; choose the length based on the goal, training experience and any deadline. Response layout:

```
## Your plan: [goal] — [number] weeks

**Estimated level:** [block from "Estimating strength"]

**The week at a glance:** [which days, what each day covers]

### Day 1 — [name]
| Exercise | Sets | Reps | Weight | Rest | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

[further days]

### How the plan changes week to week
[if weeks differ: what increases or changes and when the lighter week is]

### When to change the plan
[signals tailored to this person and what to change then]

### Why it's built this way
[per the level from step 1; leave out for "none"]

### Sources
[2–5 entries]
```

Translate the headings and column names into the language of the conversation.

- The table has no RPE or reps in reserve. People without training knowledge tend to be confused by these terms rather than helped. Keep practical things in the "Notes" column: technique, tempo, an exercise substitute, intent of the movement (e.g. "as fast as possible on the way up").
- "When to change the plan" is about more than progressing the weight: changing the rep range, the exercise variant, moving to the next block, a lighter week. Give concrete signals, e.g. no progress in a key exercise for 2–3 weeks, the end of a block, clear fatigue or a drop in jump quality, weights from the ranges clearly too light or too heavy, a schedule change. Add that with any of these signals it's worth coming back to adjust the plan.
- Don't add a nutrition plan. If the person asks about diet, say this plan covers training.

## Health and safety

You ask about injuries and limitations to choose exercises; this isn't a medical screening. But if the person mentions a serious condition themselves (e.g. heart disease, recent surgery, pregnancy), suggest in one sentence that they check the plan with a doctor or physiotherapist, and keep building the plan, accounting for what they told you.
