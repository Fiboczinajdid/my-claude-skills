# Refreshing a plan used for a long time

Read this file when the person has followed the same plan for a long time (usually several months) with few or no changes and wants to refresh or "switch up" their training: they've heard a plan should be changed regularly, progress has slowed without one clear cause, or they're bored. If they come back for a specific reason instead (weights don't fit, pain, the end of a block, a new schedule or goal, skipped sessions), use `change-plan.md`. If both apply, run this file and handle the specific reason with the signal table in `change-plan.md`.

A refresh goes through the same process as building a plan: data → research → "research → decision" note → checklist → plan. The difference is that it starts with a diagnosis, because a plan being old is not by itself a reason to change it.

## The premise, stated honestly

Research doesn't support replacing a whole plan on a calendar schedule. What it does show (sources in section 5):

- Progressive overload drives results. A plan stops working when weights or reps stop going up at the same effort, or when fatigue, boredom or pain build up, not on a date.
- Planned exercise variation can improve growth in parts of a muscle that one exercise misses; frequent random rotation can hurt progress.
- Variety raises motivation to train, and adherence matters a lot, so boredom is a legitimate reason to rotate exercises.
- Planned variation in load (periodization) beats keeping the same loading for maximal strength, but not for muscle size when weekly sets are equal.
- For most adults, consistency matters more than complexity.

Tell the person this to the extent of their chosen explanation level: the refresh keeps what still works, fixes what stalled and rotates some exercises on purpose. For "short", one or two sentences are enough; for "none", skip it.

## 1. Collect data

Same rules as in the interview (SKILL.md): batches of 3–5 questions, don't ask again about what's already been said, any question can be skipped, skipped = "no data" with no made-up values, and ask once at the end about skipped key items.

| Item | What it changes |
|---|---|
| Explanation level (none / short / detailed), if you don't know what they chose before | How much of the reasoning you show |
| The current plan, in full (pasted, described, photo) **(key)** | You can only diagnose what you can see |
| How long they've followed it unchanged | How stale the exercises and loading are likely to be |
| Weight × reps in key exercises now and a few months ago, or a training log **(key)** | Separates exercises that stalled from ones still progressing |
| Which exercises or muscles stopped improving, and since when | The changes target those, not everything |
| How hard a typical set is ("at the end of a set, how many more reps could you have done?") | Effort is a common hidden cause of stalling |
| Sessions actually done compared with planned | Real frequency and weekly sets |
| Body weight trend and whether they're trying to lose weight | Eating to lose weight can explain a stall on its own |
| Sleep, stress, other demanding activities | Recovery limits how much more volume makes sense |
| New pain or injury **(key)** | Exercise swaps |
| Last lighter week or break | Accumulated fatigue can look like a stale plan |
| Why they want a refresh: slower progress, boredom, advice they heard, something else | What a successful refresh means for them |
| Exercises they're tired of, and ones they enjoy | What to rotate and what to keep |
| Whether the goal is still the same | If not, reinterpret the goal (SKILL.md) and use `data-by-goal.md` |

## 2. Diagnose before changing anything

Treat an exercise as stalled only when the same exercise, at the same effort, has gained no weight or reps for 2–3 weeks. For each stalled exercise or lagging muscle, go through the checks in order and stop at the first one that applies. Cheap causes come first; swapping the exercise comes last.

| Check, in order | If yes |
|---|---|
| 1. Still progressing at the same effort? | Keep it unchanged |
| 2. Poor sleep, high stress, or eating to lose weight? | Say plainly that this is the likely limiter and that the plan can't fully fix it; keep volume steady rather than adding more. Diet stays outside this skill |
| 3. Sets end far from failure (4 or more reps left)? | Train closer to failure: for muscle growth, most sets end with 0–3 reps left, failure mainly on isolation and machine exercises; for heavy strength work, a few reps left is enough. Write it in plain words in the Notes column, e.g. "the last rep should be hard, at most 1–2 more possible" |
| 4. The muscle gets fewer than about 10 sets a week, counting sets where it only assists (e.g. triceps in bench press) as half? | Add 2–4 sets a week for that muscle for this block, starting close to the current volume |
| 5. Results dropping across several exercises, aches, or low motivation for 1–2 weeks? | A lighter week first: fewer sets and lighter weights, not a complete break. Then progress again |
| 6. Strength goal and the lift is trained once a week? | Add a second weekly session of that lift at moderate weight |
| 7. None of the above? | Swap for a close variant that trains the same muscle, ideally one that loads it in the stretched position; keep it for at least one full block |

If the diagnosis finds nothing that needs changing, say so, and say what to do instead (keep going, push sets harder, sort out sleep). An unchanged plan with better effort is a valid outcome of a refresh.

## 3. What to change and what to keep

- **One or two changes per stalled exercise per block**, so the person can tell what worked.
- **Keep anchor exercises.** Keep one or two main exercises per movement or muscle group for at least two blocks as the measure of progress, unless they hurt or stayed stalled after the checks above.
- **Rotate accessory exercises on purpose**, at the start of a block (every 4–8 weeks), each swap with a stated reason: a lagging part of a muscle, joint comfort, the stretched position, or enjoyment. Never rotate randomly from session to session.
- **Strength goals:** add planned variation in load, e.g. a heavier and a lighter day for the same lift, or blocks that move toward heavier weights.
- **Muscle goals:** progress weekly sets and effort within the block. The split matters less than the weekly sets it delivers.
- **Rest:** at least about 90 seconds on most sets for muscle growth, longer on heavy compound lifts.
- **Range of motion:** full range by default, with emphasis on the stretched position.
- **Boredom as the main reason:** rotate more of the accessory work and keep the anchors. Training gets fresher while progress stays measurable.
- **Lighter weeks:** when fatigue signals appear, not on a rigid schedule. Reduce sets and weights rather than stopping completely.

## 4. Research and decision

- For each variable you're changing, search the same way as when building a plan: in English, with the source hierarchy from SKILL.md, accounting for this person's population. Start from the snapshot in section 5, then check for newer meta-analyses and position stands.
- Useful search phrases (swap in the variable, exercise or muscle):
  - `resistance training weekly volume hypertrophy meta-regression`
  - `proximity to failure "repetitions in reserve" meta-analysis`
  - `"exercise variation" resistance training hypertrophy systematic review`
  - `deload resistance-trained randomized`
  - `"long muscle length" OR "lengthened partial" hypertrophy`
  - `<exercise A> vs <exercise B> hypertrophy trained`
  - `<topic> meta-analysis <current year>`
- Check each study for: trained or untrained participants, duration, whether volume was equal between groups, how muscle was measured (MRI or ultrasound beat body-composition scales), and sample size.
- Red flags: "muscle confusion" or "shock the muscle" claims, one study presented as proof, sources selling supplements or programs, short-term markers (the pump, hormone spikes) treated as long-term growth, data from untrained people applied to an experienced lifter without saying so.
- Write the "research → decision" note for the variables you're changing, including how this person actually responded to the old plan.
- Go through the checklist in SKILL.md, plus:
  - Does every change trace to the diagnosis or the person's preference, never to "it's time for a change"?
  - At most one or two changes per stalled exercise?
  - Are exercises that still progress unchanged, or is the reason for changing them written down?
  - Are weekly sets counted for the priority muscles, with volume still close to the current level?

## 5. Evidence snapshot (October 2026)

Starting points to verify, not final answers. Most studies last 6–12 weeks in young adults, often untrained, so the person's own training history takes precedence.

| Variable | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly sets | More weekly sets give more muscle growth, with gradual diminishing returns; for strength, returns flatten much sooner. Counting indirect sets as half fit the data best | [Pelland et al. 2026, Sports Medicine](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal) |
| Frequency | Little independent effect on muscle size once weekly sets are equal; a clear benefit for strength | [Pelland et al. 2026](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal) |
| General dose | Each major muscle at least twice a week; about 10 sets per muscle per week for growth; for strength, about 80% of 1RM or more, 2–3 sets per exercise | [ACSM position stand 2026](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/) |
| Load | Light and heavy loads build similar muscle when sets go close to failure; heavy loads give more 1RM strength | [Schoenfeld et al. 2017, JSCR](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/) |
| Effort | Muscle growth improves as sets end closer to failure; strength is hardly affected | [Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine](https://www.fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/muscle-growth-strength-study) |
| Rest | A small benefit to resting more than 60 s; no clear extra benefit beyond 90 s | [Singer et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/) |
| Range of motion | Full or long range tends to beat short partials; training at long muscle lengths may add growth | [Wolf et al. 2023](https://doaj.org/article/99e86b317bec40f5abc2f0008358737e) |
| Exercise variation | Planned variation can improve regional growth; excessive random rotation may hurt; variety raises motivation | [Kassiano et al. 2022, Fonseca et al. 2014, Baz-Valle et al. 2019](https://getfitcraft.com/science/muscle-confusion-myth) |
| Periodization | Better for 1RM strength than non-periodized training; no difference for size when volume is equal | [Moesgaard et al. 2022, Sports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/) |
| Lighter week | A full week off mid-block gave similar growth but slightly less strength gain, and participants felt flat afterwards | [Coleman et al. 2024, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/16777) |

## 6. Show the changes

1. **Diagnosis:** a few sentences on what stalled, the likely cause, and what still works.
2. **What changes and why:** a short list, to the extent of the chosen explanation level. For "none", the list of changes alone is enough.
3. **The full updated plan**, in the same format as in SKILL.md, with changed items marked (e.g. "(changed)" in the Notes column).
4. **When to change the plan**, for the new version, including a check-in halfway through the block and at its end: compare weight × reps in the anchor exercises with the start of the block.
5. **Sources**, for the evidence you used in this refresh.
