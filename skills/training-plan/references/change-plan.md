# Changing an existing plan

Read this file when the person comes back with a plan and wants to change it for a specific reason: the weights don't fit, a key exercise has stalled, a block has ended, they feel run down, something hurts, their time, equipment or schedule has changed, their goal has changed, or they keep skipping sessions. If the main reason is that they've followed the same plan for a long time and want to refresh it, use `refresh-plan.md` instead.

A change goes through **the same process as building a plan**: data → research → "research → decision" note → checklist → plan. There's one difference: change only what the reason requires, and leave the parts of the plan that work as they are. That gives the person continuity, makes results easier to compare, and keeps the changes clear. Make one or two changes per problem, so the person can tell what helped.

## 1. Collect data

- **The reason for the change,** in the person's own words.
- **The current plan.** If it isn't in the conversation, ask them to paste or describe it (a photo works).
- **Explanation level** (none / short / detailed), if you don't know what they chose before.
- **What happened:** how long they've been on the plan; weight × reps in key exercises, ideally from the first and the last week; results in tests tied to the goal (e.g. vertical jump, 5 km time); which exercises went well and which badly; how many sessions they actually did.
- **How they feel:** fatigue, sleep, pain, new injuries.
- **What changed:** time, equipment, schedule, goal, other activities, race date.

Same rules as in the interview: batches of 3–5 questions, don't ask again about what's already been said, any question can be skipped, skipped = "no data" with no made-up values. If a skipped item is key to a decision, ask once at the end whether they're sure they want to skip it. For a new goal or a new motor quality, use `data-by-goal.md`.

## 2. Recognize the signals

When several signals appear at once, handle them in this order: pain or injury → changed circumstances or goal → fatigue → weights that don't fit → stalls and the end of a block. Safety comes first, then the constraints the plan has to fit, then fine-tuning.

| Signal | What to consider |
|---|---|
| Pain or a new injury | Swapping exercises that load that area for ones that don't hurt, and keep training the rest; for a serious condition, apply the safety rule from SKILL.md |
| Less time per session or fewer days | Keeping the weekly sets for the main goal's priority muscles and cutting secondary goals first. For muscle growth, fewer and longer sessions can deliver the same weekly sets, since frequency adds little once sets are equal; for strength, keep practicing the key lifts at least twice a week |
| Different equipment or location | Swapping for exercises that train the same muscles and movement through a similar range of motion; recalculating weights cautiously |
| New schedule | Rebuilding the weekly split around the available days while keeping the goal's priorities and spacing hard sessions for the same muscles |
| Changed goal or priorities | Reinterpreting the goal (motor qualities, main and secondary goals, conflicts) and confirming with the person |
| The person skips some sessions | Asking why (time, fatigue, motivation, disliked exercises), since each needs a different fix; then a plan fitted to their real availability instead of an ideal one that isn't being done |
| Clear fatigue, worse sleep, dropping results, worse jump or sprint quality | A lighter week: fewer sets and lighter weights rather than a complete break, then further progression. In one study a full week off gave no extra growth and slightly less strength gain |
| Weights from the ranges clearly too light (they do more reps than the top of the range in every set) | Increasing the weight by the smallest sensible increment for that exercise and recalculating the ranges from their actual results. Give a progression rule going forward: reach the top of the rep range in all sets, then add weight |
| Weights clearly too heavy (they don't reach the bottom of the rep range, or technique breaks down) | Reducing the weight; first check whether fatigue is the cause (then see above) |
| No progress in a key exercise for 2–3 weeks | Going through the causes in order before changing the exercise: recovery (sleep, stress, eating to lose weight) → effort (sets ending far from failure) → weekly sets for that muscle → fatigue across several exercises → for strength, how often the lift is trained. Change the rep range, the variant or the volume only after that. The full order is in section 2 of `refresh-plan.md` |
| End of a block (planned period) | Moving to the next phase, e.g. from building volume to higher intensity, or a new block for the next stage of the goal. For strength goals, plan variation in load (heavier blocks, or heavier and lighter days). For muscle goals, restart weekly sets near the previous block's starting level and rotate a few accessory exercises for a stated reason |

The table is a starting point for thinking, not a ready decision. Choose the specific solution based on research and this person's situation.

## 3. Research and decision

- For each variable you're considering changing, search for research the same way as when building the plan: in English, with the source hierarchy (meta-analyses and systematic reviews → ACSM and NSCA position stands → single studies → blogs only with a note), accounting for this person's population.
- Fit the scope of the search to the changes: adjusting a weight from actual results usually needs no new research, while changing the phase, the volume, the split or how goals are combined does.
- For volume, frequency, effort, rest, exercise variation, periodization and lighter weeks, section 5 of `refresh-plan.md` has a dated evidence snapshot and section 4 has search phrases. Use them as a starting point and check for newer research.
- Write the "research → decision" note for the variables you're changing. Include what research can't provide: how this person actually responded to the plan so far.
- If you decide nothing needs to change, say so plainly and explain why. Change for its own sake isn't optimal.
- Go through the checklist from SKILL.md for the updated plan, and also check that every change traces to the reason the person gave or a signal you found.

## 4. Show the changes

1. **What changes and why:** a short list of changes tied to the reason, to the extent of the chosen explanation level. For "none", the list of changes alone is enough.
2. **The full updated plan**, in the same format as in SKILL.md, with changed items marked (e.g. "(changed)" in the "Notes" column). The person then has one current version instead of two to piece together.
3. **When to change the plan**, for the new version.
4. **Sources**, if you used new ones for this change.
